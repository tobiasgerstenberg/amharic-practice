Amharic Practice — አማርኛ

Stanford Beginning Amharic · summary of class notes (9/23 – 10/5/2026) + writing & reading exercises

The big ideas so far

  • Consonants and vowels go together. Each character (fidel) is a whole syllable: one consonant + one vowel.
  • Each consonant has 7 forms, called “houses” (orders). The 1st house is the Ge'ez (base) form, and the other six change it in a regular way.
  • The alphabet has about 200+ characters. Your class groups them by their “legs” (see the pyramid).
  • There is no upper or lower case.
  • “If you can't hear the vowel, it's the 6th house.” In your notes: | = a syllable with a vowel you hear, \ = 6th house (little or no vowel).
  • Word order is Subject – Object – Verb (SOV), so the verb comes last.
  • Sentences end with ። (four dots, a full stop). Questions use ? (also written ፧) and a rising voice.

How to build a number

  • Tens, then ones: 47 = ፵ + ፯ = ፵፯.
  • Hundreds: put the count in front of ፻: 300 = ፫፻, 365 = ፫፻፷፭. 100 alone is just ፻, without ፩.
  • Thousands are counted in hundreds: 1,000 = ፲፻ (10 × 100), and 2,500 = ፳፭፻ (25 × 100).
  • Ten thousand has its own symbol ፼, and it works like ፻: 20,000 = ፪፼.
  • Ethiopians use these symbols mostly for dates, years, and chapter or page numbers. Prices and phone numbers use 1 2 3.

How past-tense verb endings work

Amharic past-tense verbs follow one pattern: start from the “he” form, then change the ending to show who did it. Because the ending already says who, you can usually leave out the pronoun. በላ on its own means “he ate”, and በላን on its own means “we ate”.

The starting point is “he ___ed”. The dictionary form of a verb is the “he did it” past form: በላ he ate, ገዛ he bought, ሰበረ he broke, ጻፈ he wrote. To make other people the subject, you change the house of the last character, and for most persons you also add an ending.

In the tables, the last character of the verb is colored by its house: ■ 1st ■ 2nd ■ 4th ■ 6th. The added ending is bold. Press 🔊 to hear a form.

Type 1: verbs that end in the 1st house (‑e), like ሰበረ

Which house does the last character go into?

  • An ending that starts with a consonant (‑ku, ‑k, ‑sh, ‑n) puts it in the 6th house (no vowel). Then you add the ending: ሰበርኩ.
  • ‑u (“they”) is the 2nd house, and nothing is added: ሰበሩ.
  • ‑achihu (“you all”) starts with the 4th house: ሰበራችሁ.
  • “He” and “she” keep the 1st house. “She” just adds ች: ሰበረች.

Type 2: verbs that end in the 4th house (‑a), like በላ

Many common verbs end in ‑a (በላ, ገዛ, መጣ, ገባ, ሰማ, ተኛ). They use the same endings, with one change: after the ‑a, the “k” of “I” and “you (m.)” turns into “h” (‑hu, ‑h).

The last character already ends in a vowel, so you just add the ending. The exception is “they”, where ‑a becomes ‑u (ላ → ሉ, ዛ → ዙ).

All the endings at a glance

Memory tricks

  • I = ‑ku / ‑hu, you = ‑k / ‑h, you (f.) = ‑sh. These are the same sounds as in አንቺ / ‑ሽ, and the “you” ending matches እባክህ / እባክሽ (“please” to a man / woman).
  • We = ‑n, just like እኛ ነን (“we are”).
  • She = ‑ch. It's the only person that adds an ending but keeps the 1st house.
  • They = ‑u, and the same form is used to talk politely about or to someone. That's why the flashcards say “they / he, she (polite) ate” for በሉ.
  • You (all) = ‑achihu, which sounds like እናንተ … ናችሁ (“you all are”).

Two things the script doesn't show

  • Doubled consonants aren't written. በላ is said bella, with a long l, but it's written with a single ላ. That's why the word list shows “bela” and the sentence diagram shows “bella”.
  • This covers only the simple past tense. The present / future form (for example ይበላል, “he eats / will eat”) puts prefixes in front of the verb as well. It follows a separate pattern, which is a good next step once these endings feel natural.
